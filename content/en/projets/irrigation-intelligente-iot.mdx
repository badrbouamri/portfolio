## Context

An innovation project built by a team of seven Industrial Systems Mechanical Engineering students at ENSET Mohammedia, supervised by Mr. Lakhdar Abdelghani and Mr. Samri Hassan. The goal: design a smart, autonomous irrigation system based on ESP32, MQTT and artificial intelligence, for home or small-plot use, where irrigation today is triggered manually or on a simple timer, without accounting for actual soil conditions or upcoming weather.

## Problem

A fixed-timer watering schedule ignores two pieces of information that are readily available: actual soil moisture and upcoming rainfall. As a result, a non-connected system either over-waters (wasting water) or under-waters (water stress), with no data available afterward to verify which.

## Objectives

- Automate irrigation triggering from a local measurement (soil moisture), rather than a fixed timer.
- Integrate a weather forecast (OpenWeather API) to avoid watering before expected rain.
- Enable remote supervision and control of the system.

## My role

A project built by a team of seven students. I was involved across the whole technical chain: the embedded firmware on the ESP32 (sensors, relay, Wi-Fi and MQTT connectivity), the MQTT communication layer between the ESP32, the AI server, and the mobile app, the Random Forest decision model, and the Flutter mobile supervision app.

## Approach & methodology

The chosen architecture follows a six-step chain:

1. **Local acquisition** — the ESP32 reads a soil moisture sensor and a DHT11 sensor (air temperature and humidity).
2. **Weather enrichment** — the ESP32 queries the OpenWeatherMap API (current rain, 24h and 48h forecasts) and merges it with the local readings.
3. **MQTT publication** — the combined data (sensors + weather) is published as JSON to a Mosquitto broker, on the `irrigation/sensors` topic.
4. **AI decision** — a server (PC) hosting a Random Forest model (trained with scikit-learn, saved in joblib format) receives this data over MQTT and decides whether to irrigate.
5. **Action** — the decision is republished on the `irrigation/command` topic; the ESP32, subscribed to it, activates a relay driving a 5V motor that simulates opening the valve. The valve's state is in turn published on `irrigation/valve`.
6. **Remote supervision** — a mobile app built with Flutter subscribes to the data and state topics for real-time display, and publishes to `irrigation/command` for manual commands.

MQTT (rather than classic HTTP polling) was chosen for its lightweight publish/subscribe model, suited to a resource-constrained device like the ESP32.

## Tools & methods

`ESP32` · `MQTT` · `OpenWeather API` · `Random Forest` · `Flutter` · soil moisture sensor · DHT11

## Engineering analysis

Three design choices deserve justification, and one measured result needs a caveat.

**MQTT rather than direct HTTP polling.** A resource-constrained connected device benefits from minimising the number and duration of active network connections. MQTT's publish/subscribe model lets the ESP32 publish readings and receive commands asynchronously, instead of continuously polling or being polled — reducing power consumption and network load, a criterion explicitly stated in the requirements (the energy-efficiency non-functional requirement).

**A trained model rather than a simple threshold.** The simplest initial hypothesis — irrigate below a fixed soil-moisture threshold — was ruled out in favour of a Random Forest model that combines several variables (soil moisture, temperature, air humidity, current rain, 24h and 48h forecasts) to decide. A single threshold ignores upcoming weather; the model, for instance, can skip irrigation despite dry soil if rain is forecast in the coming hours.

**Trained on synthetic data, not field measurements.** With no real data available at the time of the project, the model was trained on a synthetic dataset generated to simulate different combinations of moisture, temperature and weather, then evaluated on an equally simulated test set — with an accuracy above 95% on that test set. This result validates the model's internal consistency against the simulated scenarios; it doesn't guarantee the same accuracy against field situations not represented in training — a limitation the team owns rather than hides. Every decision made in operation is logged (CSV) to allow future retraining on real data.

## Challenges encountered

Hardware integration required several wiring adjustments between the sensors and the ESP32 to get a stable reading and limit electrical interference.

The lack of available real data forced the team to rely on simulated data to train the AI model — a deliberate choice, but one that limits how much confidence can be placed in its accuracy on field situations not represented in training.

Securing the MQTT broker (TLS/SSL) took longer than expected, between configuring certificates and managing permissions. Some latency was also observed at times between sensor readings being sent and their display in the mobile app, requiring code optimisation.

## Solutions implemented

- ESP32 firmware: sensor readings, weather API calls, MQTT publish/subscribe, relay control, automatic reconnection after a Wi-Fi or MQTT drop.
- A Random Forest decision model (scikit-learn), trained on simulated data and saved in joblib format, with decision logging for future retraining.
- A Flutter mobile app: real-time display of readings, valve-state tracking, manual override.
- An MQTT broker (Mosquitto) secured with TLS/SSL between the three modules.

## Results

The prototype passed every functional test run: sensor readings conformant, reliable relay switching, MQTT publish/subscribe with no message loss, automatic reconnection after a Wi-Fi/MQTT drop or a power cut, mobile app working across several Android smartphones. The decision model reached over 95% accuracy on its test set — a simulated dataset, not a real-world validation.

**No quantified water-consumption gain is available**: this project didn't undergo a real-world before/after comparative measurement, and the prototype isn't yet fitted with an autonomous power source (battery or solar panel) or a housing suited to prolonged outdoor use. Presenting it as a functional, tested prototype, rather than putting forward an unmeasured water saving, is deliberate.

## What I take from this

Working across the whole technical chain — firmware, communication, AI and app — taught me to reason about a complete system rather than an isolated module: latency in the mobile app could come from the firmware, the MQTT broker, or the network, and you have to locate the problem in the right layer before fixing it.

Training the model on synthetic data is the choice I'd make differently today: I'd go find some real field measurements, even a small amount, before quoting an accuracy figure, rather than validating the model only against simulated scenarios that just test its internal consistency.
